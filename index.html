Indoor localization is hard without line of sight

A receiver moves through a building and must find its position from the radio signal it receives.

Line of sight (LOS)

Received power falls predictably with distance, so a simple model locates the receiver.

Non-line of sight (NLOS)

Walls and corners add unknown losses. The simple model fails and localization becomes difficult.

We study the NLOS case: a receiver in a corridor the transmitter cannot see.

30 s. "Indoors there is no satellite positioning, so a receiver has to answer 'Where am I?' from the radio signals around it. With line of sight to the transmitter, received power falls predictably with distance and localization is manageable. Once the transmitter is out of sight, walls and corners add losses we do not know, and localization becomes difficult. That is the case we study."

This is the general contrast. LOS localization still has multipath; the point is that a distance model is a usable start there and stops being one in NLOS.

In the paper, NLOS describes the transmitter: TX-to-tag illumination is NLOS, and the receiver is outside direct transmitter illumination. Tag-to-RX links are mostly LOS. Slide 2 shows that geometry. Do not go into it here.

Path, blue: the track is a sequence of cell indices, ℓ₁ to ℓ_T, so a dynamic program can search it exactly. A step stays in the cell or moves to a neighbour within 0.6 m.

If asked why 0.4 m cells: the observation model is a large-scale propagation model. It describes mean power, a distance trend plus a quasi-static correction per cell, and leaves small-scale fading in the noise term. So a cell should span about a wavelength or more: 0.4 m is about 1.15 λ at 866 MHz (λ = 34.6 cm). Smaller cells would ask the model to resolve fading it does not describe.

If asked whether the cell size limits accuracy: refining 0.4 to 0.3 m moves the median by 2 mm (backup B2).

The model turns each sample into a cost per cell

Observation cost \(c_t(\ell)\)

How far the measured tag powers are from what the model predicts for cell \(\ell\), summed over the tags.

Motion penalty \(m(\ell', \ell)\)

The price of a step from cell \(\ell'\) to cell \(\ell\). A longer step costs more.

The estimated path is the cell sequence with the lowest total cost.

35 s, no clicks. "How does a path come out of this model? Take one sample: four measured powers, one per tag. For any cell, the model says what those four powers would be if the receiver were there. The observation cost is the mismatch, summed over the tags. Dark cells explain the sample well."

"We do not pick the best cell sample by sample. A motion penalty charges for long steps, and the estimate is the cell sequence with the lowest total cost. That is the search the dynamic program does."

Paper: in stage 1 the observation cost is a Huber penalty (4 dB scale) on z − Δ − G, with the residual map set to zero. In stage 3 it is the Gaussian negative log-likelihood with the map-corrected mean and a variance per carrier, cell and tag. Dual band adds the costs of the two carriers with weights.

Motion: m(ℓ′, ℓ) = λ · Huber(step length), with λ = 5 and a 0.4 m Huber scale. Only cells within 0.6 m are allowed as predecessors. Staying in the cell costs nothing.

The shading is illustrative, not measured data. It is the 4 dB Huber cost of the distance trend with slope 2 and d0 = 0.1 m, for a noise-free sample taken in the outlined cell (x = 0, y = 7.15 m). The tag offsets cancel in this construction. Neighbouring cells score almost as well as the outlined one.

The equation first: "Everything on this slide is one minimisation. A path is one grid cell per sample. Its total cost is the observation cost of the cell chosen for each sample, plus a motion penalty for each step between consecutive cells. The estimate is the path with the lowest total. Dynamic programming finds that minimum exactly; it is the Viterbi algorithm."

Stage 1 is a tracker in its own right: R = 0, alternate the dynamic program with a refit of p_f and G with a Huber loss, at most 5 iterations. No measured calibration positions are required. The reported dual-band cold-start result learns its carrier weight from a provisional equal-weight profiling pass on the 46 unlabeled calibration runs.

Stage 2 is where "self-calibrated" comes from: the reference path is produced by the algorithm, so the calibration runs are unlabeled. It runs no dynamic program. It refits p_f and G on that path, bins the residuals per grid cell, smooths the cell means and recentres them, so that the constant level stays in G. The cell variances are smoothed and clipped in the same way.

The map is estimated from the 46 unlabeled calibration runs and then applied to the 46 evaluation runs. It is not built online during a run.

Stage 3 reruns the same dynamic program. Only the observation cost changes; the motion penalty is the same in stages 1 and 3.

If asked how the minimum is found: D_t(ℓ) = c_t(ℓ) + min over ℓ′ in N(ℓ) of [ D_{t−1}(ℓ′) + m(ℓ′, ℓ) ], starting from D_1(ℓ) = c_1(ℓ). D_t(ℓ) is the lowest total cost of any path that ends in cell ℓ at sample t, and N(ℓ) is the set of cells within 0.6 m of ℓ. After the last sample, the cell with the lowest D ends the path, and tracing the chosen predecessors back gives the whole path (Viterbi backtracking).

The same stages 2 and 3 can use measured reference coordinates from timestamp interpolation. This route-matched reference shows the remaining gap when using a surrogate; it is not an independent surveyed ground truth.

"Both observation costs compare the same two numbers: the power measured from tag k, and the power the model predicts if the receiver were in cell ℓ. That prediction is the model of 5.1 without its error term."

Stage 1: "Before there is a map, the mismatch goes through a Huber penalty: quadratic up to 4 dB and linear beyond, so a deep fade on one tag cannot decide the cell." Paper: this robustifies the fit against deep fades, anchor-specific outliers and strong local multipath distortion. p_f and G are those of the current refit.

Stage 3: "Once stage 2 has given the map and a spread per cell, the cost is the Gaussian negative log-likelihood: the squared mismatch over twice the variance, plus log σ." The log σ term stops a cell from scoring well only because its spread is large. The residuals are treated as independent across tags. σ is clipped for numerical stability. In the constant-variance variant, σ is one pooled value per carrier and tag.

Motion: a Huber penalty on the distance between the two cell centres, with λ = 5 and a 0.4 m scale. On the 0.4 m grid, staying costs 0, a step to an edge neighbour 0.40 and a diagonal step 0.73. Cells more than 0.6 m apart are not connected, so the path cannot jump. Stage 2 runs no dynamic program, so the penalty is used in stages 1 and 3.

Two carriers: a weighted sum of the two per-carrier costs. The weight on 899 MHz is 0.5 in the provisional profiling pass and about 0.53 after it, for the unlabeled and surrogate cases. The common-mode variant also profiles out a per-sample shift β_t; its cost is on backup slide B4.

The symbols carry no hats here. The paper writes the stage 3 plug-in values as μ̂ and σ̂, as backup slide B4 does.

For "what about single band / P95 / bias?"

Single band, surrogate map: 866 MHz median 0.505 to 0.497 m, P90 gets worse (0.93 to 1.01 m); the gain is bias (0.20 to 0.01 m).

RADAR with measured reference labels beats us in single band (0.31 vs 0.41 m). Our main claim is tracking without measured calibration positions; the measured-coordinate results are route-matched references.

For "how sensitive is this to tuning?" and "is the error just the grid spacing?"

Refining the grid changes the median by 2 mm, so the grid is not the limit.

Why 0.4 m: the observation model is a large-scale propagation model, so a cell spans about one wavelength (34.6 cm at 866 MHz) and small-scale fading stays in the noise term.

Block averaging mainly helps the tail.

Stage 2: separating the tag offset from the map

  • Residuals on the reference path: \(e_{f,k,t} = z_{f,k,t} - \Delta_f(\tilde U_t, B_k; \hat p_f) - \hat G_{f,k}\)
  • Each sample goes to its nearest grid cell; cell means are smoothed over neighbours, weighted by sample count
  • Adding a constant to the map and subtracting it from \(G_{f,k}\) changes nothing, so the map is recentred: \(\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal V} a_\ell\, \hat r_{f,\ell,k} = 0\)
  • The variance map is built the same way and clipped to 1–12 dB

Paths stay near the centreline, so off-axis cells are weakly constrained: the map is a correction along the corridor strip, not a full two-dimensional field.

For "how do you separate G from R?"

a_ℓ is proportional to the number of calibration samples in cell ℓ.

Common-mode term

Innovations \(\eta_{f,k,t}(\ell) = z_{f,k,t} - \hat\mu_{f,\ell,k}\) share a per-sample shift \(\beta_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_b^2)\).

\(\displaystyle \hat\beta_{t,\ell} = \frac{\sum_{f}\sum_{k} \omega_{f,\ell,k}\, \eta_{f,k,t}(\ell)} {\sum_{f}\sum_{k} \omega_{f,\ell,k} + \sigma_b^{-2}}, \qquad \omega_{f,\ell,k} = \frac{w_f}{\hat\sigma_{f,\ell,k}^2}\)
\(\displaystyle c_t^{\mathrm{dual+com}}(\ell) = \tfrac12 \sum_{f}\sum_{k} \omega_{f,\ell,k} \big(\eta_{f,k,t}(\ell) - \hat\beta_{t,\ell}\big)^2 + \sum_{f}\sum_{k} w_f \log \hat\sigma_{f,\ell,k} + \frac{\hat\beta_{t,\ell}^2}{2\sigma_b^2}\)

Used as a robust scoring rule, not as a fully normalized joint likelihood.

For "is the common-mode cost a proper likelihood?" No, and the paper says so.

A full channel-wise cross-band covariance model is future work.
